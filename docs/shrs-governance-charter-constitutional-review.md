# CONSTITUTIONAL REVIEW — THE GOVERNANCE CHARTER OF SULTAN HANAFI ROYAL SCHOOLS

*Conducted in response to the Final Constitutional Review Commission Directive. This is a companion review document, not part of the Charter, carrying no legal effect of its own. It records what was checked, what was found, what was fixed, and what was deliberately left for a future Statute, Regulation, Policy, or the future Constitution referred to in Article 178.*

---

## Methodology, stated plainly

The directive asked for independent thinking and merciless self-critique — not a restatement of confidence in work already done. To make that real rather than nominal, the review ran in two passes:

**Pass one — genuinely independent.** A separate reviewing agent was given only the raw Charter text (`docs/shrs-constitution-2026-draft.md`) and told explicitly *not* to read this Charter's own companion Editorial Record or any other file in this repository — because that record contains this drafter's own rationale for every choice made across twenty-two prior rounds, and reading it first would have anchored the review toward agreeing with decisions already made. That agent returned 25 findings, sorted by severity, each required to name the specific Article(s), the problem, a concrete scenario (not a hypothetical generality), and a severity rating.

**Pass two — verification, not rubber-stamping.** Every finding was then checked personally against the actual Charter text, Article by Article, before being accepted. This mattered: it surfaced that one finding (Board-seat removal under Article 37 versus office removal under Article 23A) had already been explicitly anticipated and distinguished in the Charter's own text, which the independent reviewer had characterised as an unaddressed gap rather than a real but narrower gap around disqualifying grounds specifically — the finding was real, but its precise shape needed correcting before it could be fixed correctly. Two further findings (Article 43's "unanimous consent" single-holdout risk, and the entrenchment-workaround chain through Article 7) were sharpened or reframed based on direct re-reading rather than accepted as first presented.

**A third, independent pass** then applied the directive's own "think independently" checklist (governance principles, reserved powers, delegation, succession, vacancies, acting appointments, continuity, Board procedure, committees, ethics, conflicts, confidentiality, safeguarding, audit, risk, financial accountability, interpretation, amendment, repeal, saving clauses, symbols, official language, records, seal, review, emergency governance) against the full Charter text independently of the adversarial agent's own findings, which surfaced two further genuine gaps not on the agent's list (Findings 26 and 27 below).

**What this review is not.** Not a certification by King's Counsel, Oxford or Cambridge governance experts, Saudi Ministry of Education specialists, UNESCO advisers, or any accreditation body — this drafter holds none of those credentials, and no claim to the contrary is made anywhere in this document. Every finding below is checkable against the actual Charter text cited; nothing here should be read as, or substitute for, real legal review before Board adoption.

## STAGE ONE — CLASSIFICATION

### Category A — Critical (corrected before adoption, this round)

Every item below was verified against the Charter's actual text, found genuine, and has been fixed in this round — see Stage Four for the exact amendment. Numbering follows the order the findings were verified in, not severity within the category (all ten are treated as adoption-blocking).

**A1 — The entrenchment protecting Article 8 can be worked around through Article 7 and Article 43.** Article 8 (Islamic character) is entrenched at a 4/5 Board vote (Article 157). But the Institution's actual vision, mission, motto, core values, and *objects* are stated to be "adopted from time to time by the Board on the recommendation of the Founder & CEO" and merely incorporated by reference (Article 7) — not restated in, or protected by, the Charter's own entrenched text. A change to the Institution's objects is an ordinary Article 46(b) Board-approval matter, not a Chapter XVIII constitutional amendment and not on Article 45's reserved list — meaning nothing in the Charter's own text stopped it from being resolved same-day by unanimous written consent under Article 43, without notice, without debate, and without the 4/5 threshold Article 8 itself requires for a change to the Institution's stated Islamic character. **Scenario:** a Board revises the Institution's stated objects and core values in a single Article 43 resolution, materially diluting the Institution's religious mission, while Article 8's own text and its 4/5 protection remain untouched and technically unamended. **Fixed:** Article 43 now excludes changes to Article 7 (objects, vision, mission, core values, motto) from its same-day unanimous-consent mechanism, on the same footing as a constitutional amendment.

**A2 — "The objects stated in Article 3" and "the religious character in Article 2(c)" cite Articles that do not contain them.** Article 3 is entirely about the Institution's external legal corporate form; it states no objects. Article 2 has no lettered subsections at all. Yet Article 27C(a) (the Founder's approval right over changes to the Institution's objects), Article 49A (the fiduciary benchmark every Governor is held to), and Article 146A (the substantive test gating creation of a new Constituent Institution) all cite these non-existent textual anchors. As drafted, each of these three provisions could be argued unenforceable for want of a real referent. **Fixed:** all three now cite Article 7 (where "objects" genuinely appears) and Article 8 (the Institution's actual religious-character Article) respectively.

**A3 — A Founder & CEO struck by sudden, total incapacity with no advance instrument can deadlock the Board indefinitely.** Board quorum (Article 39) requires the Founder & CEO *or* an Acting Founder & CEO to be present. Appointing an Acting Founder & CEO during incapacity (Article 26) itself requires a Board resolution, which itself needs that same quorum. If incapacity is sudden and total, with no Article 25 instrument or Article 27E representative already on file, the Board cannot lawfully convene the meeting that would fix the very problem — because the person whose presence quorum depends on is exactly the person who is incapacitated, and no Acting Founder & CEO exists yet to substitute. Article 149's rescue clause does not reach this scenario: it is triggered only by an emergency affecting *multiple* Governors, not a single incapacitated Founder & CEO. **Fixed:** Article 39 now includes a narrow, purpose-limited carve-out — quorum's Founder & CEO/Acting Founder & CEO proviso does not apply to a meeting convened solely to appoint an Acting Founder & CEO under Article 26, breaking the deadlock without opening a general bypass of the ordinary quorum rule.

**A4 — No mechanism exists to appoint a *permanent* successor Founder & CEO once the founding Founder's own nomination power is no longer available.** Article 139's personal nomination power belongs to "the Founder" as defined in Article 4(c) — a specific, one-time natural person. It does not revive for a second-generation transition. Article 141 obliges the Board to run "a full search... within one year" after an interim appointment, but Chapter XVI never states who actually confirms a *permanent* successor once that search concludes, or by what vote — a real structural gap that directly undercuts the Preamble's own promise of governance "generation after generation." **Fixed:** a new Article 141A gives the Board itself the power to appoint a permanent successor, by two-thirds resolution, once Article 141's search is complete or where no Article 139 nomination is or ever again will be available.

**A5 — The Safeguarding Committee cannot lawfully meet on a concern about its own chair.** Quorum (Article 123) is defined as "the DSL and not fewer than two other members" — the DSL's presence is structurally mandatory. If the safeguarding concern is about the DSL personally, the one body whose entire purpose (Article 117, reinforced by Article 121's "notwithstanding any other provision" override) is to act regardless of who is implicated cannot convene, because it cannot reach quorum without the very person under suspicion. This is the most serious single gap found in the whole Charter, given the Institution's real child population. **Fixed:** Article 123 now provides that where a matter concerns the DSL personally, the DSL recuses from it and the senior Deputy DSL chairs and is counted toward quorum in that specific matter; Articles 120(d) and 124 are amended in step so the report and its confidentiality run to the Chair of the Board directly, bypassing the Founder & CEO, on a matter that concerns the Founder & CEO personally.

**A6 — The Chief Executive Officer's Article 14(c) veto, read literally, can block the Board's own entrenched amendment procedure for Article 8.** Article 14(c) reserves to the CEO office "veto over any resolution materially affecting the Institution's Islamic character or founding mission" — with no stated exception. A resolution to amend Article 8 under Article 157 is, on its face, exactly such a resolution. Left as written, this lets any future CEO (not only the Founder) unilaterally block the Board's own supermajority-protected amendment mechanism — a broader block than even the entrenched clause itself contemplates, and in real tension with Board supremacy under Articles 2 and 9A(a). **Fixed:** Article 14(c) now expressly excludes a Chapter XVIII amendment resolution from its scope; Chapter XVIII, including Article 157's entrenched procedure, governs Charter amendment exclusively.

**A7 — The Founder & CEO's unilateral emergency power carries none of the safeguards the Board's own emergency mechanism has.** Article 43 (the Board's emergency-resolution power) explicitly excludes matters reserved to the Board under Article 45. Article 21 (the Founder & CEO's unilateral emergency power) has no equivalent exclusion — and because Article 45 is framed as a bar on *delegating* those matters rather than an independent cap on every source of authority, a bad-faith reading could let a Founder & CEO invoke "institutional continuity" to act unilaterally on budget, external-auditor, or merger/dissolution matters Article 45 reserves exclusively to the Board. **Fixed:** Article 21 now expressly excludes Article 45 reserved matters, directing genuinely urgent Board-reserved business to Article 149 instead.

**A8 — A criminal conviction serious enough to disqualify someone from being a Governor at all does not, on its own, meet the higher bar for removing that same person from the office of Founder & CEO.** Article 31(a)'s disqualifying grounds (a conviction for dishonesty or an offence against a child) are not among Article 23A's stated grounds for CEO removal (breach of Articles 128/130/131, or a Disciplinary & Ethics Committee finding). A person could lose their Board seat under the easier Article 37 process for exactly this kind of conviction while continuing, unaffected, to run the entire Institution as Chief Executive Officer — retaining every Article 14 reserved power — unless the separate, harder Article 23A process is also independently pursued and succeeds. Given the Institution's real, current child population, this is not a theoretical gap. **Fixed:** Article 23A's removal grounds now expressly include an Article 31(a) disqualifying matter alongside breach of Articles 128/130/131.

**A9 — Article 27C promises a "reversal" of an Article 23A removal that Article 23A itself never defines, and which its own succession trigger arguably forecloses.** Article 27C's closing sentence contemplates restoring the Founder's suspended powers by a further 3/4 resolution "reversing" the removal — but Article 23A has no reversal procedure at all, and its own final sentence treats a removal as triggering full Chapter XVI succession, the same machinery used for death or permanent incapacity, categories not normally undone once a successor is appointed. Left as written, the Charter simultaneously treats a removal as final and reversible without saying how the two are reconciled. **Fixed:** Article 23A now states its own reversal procedure explicitly, including what happens to any interim or successor Founder & CEO appointed in the interim.

**A10 — Non-voting Co-opted Governors could silently raise every supermajority threshold in the Charter beyond its stated fraction.** Articles 23A, 157, and 80D each compute their supermajority against "Governors then in office" — a headcount that, as written, includes Co-opted Governors who are barred from voting on a given matter outside their expertise (Article 30(c)). A Board with enough non-voting Co-opted members could make these protections mathematically harder to reach than stated, in either direction — obstructing a legitimate removal, or making a Founder's own entrenched protections easier to erode, depending on which way the Board happens to be composed at the time. **Fixed:** Article 40 (Voting procedures) now states that a Co-opted Governor not entitled to vote on a given matter is excluded from the "Governors then in office" base for that matter's threshold.

### Category B — Important (should be improved; fixed where cheap and safe, recorded where not)

**B1 — Article 49B cites a subsection of Article 41 that does not exist.** Article 41 ("Ordinary meetings") is a single sentence about meeting frequency; it has no lettered subparts, let alone a "(d)." The right it purports to anchor — a Governor's entitlement to have a dissent recorded in the minutes — had no real textual home anywhere in the Charter. **Fixed:** the right is now stated properly in Article 40 (the natural home, since it concerns voting), and Article 49B's citation corrected to match.

**B2 — Article 134 cites "Chapters XIII, XV, and XVI" for vacancy provisions; Chapter XV is Financial Governance and contains none.** The evident intended reference is Chapter XVII (Institutional Continuity), which actually contains the vacancy and continuity provisions the sentence is describing. **Fixed:** citation corrected.

**B3 — Article 26's "temporary" incapacity is renewable indefinitely with no maximum duration and no stated trigger for converting it to "permanent" incapacity under Article 140(b).** A Board could, in theory, keep renewing a 90-day Acting appointment indefinitely rather than ever formally confirming permanent incapacity and triggering full succession — leaving the Institution in a prolonged interim state with none of Chapter XVI's permanent-succession safeguards engaged. **Fixed:** Article 26 now caps cumulative renewal at eighteen months, after which the Governance & Nominations Committee must recommend to the Board whether the incapacity should be treated as permanent.

**B4 — No procedure governs the transition to a future Constitution under Article 178.** Article 178 records the *relationship* between this Charter and a possible future, broader Constitution, but never states what vote or process would actually adopt one — a real gap in a Charter this careful everywhere else about procedure. **Fixed:** Article 178 now requires the same procedure as a Chapter XVIII amendment (and, where Article 8's substance would be affected, Article 157's entrenched procedure) for adopting a future Constitution that would supersede or subordinate this Charter — closing what the directive's own checklist called "repeal."

**B5 — Slow-motion Board attrition toward the five-Governor floor is not addressed**, distinct from Article 149's acute multi-Governor-emergency carve-out. **Recorded, not amended this round.** The Governance & Nominations Committee's existing Article 38 reporting duty on any vacancy already surfaces this before it becomes acute; a hard bootstrap-override mechanism for the theoretical case where replenishment itself becomes unreachable would add real complexity for a scenario meaningfully less likely than the ones fixed above, and risks solving a problem that hasn't actually occurred at the cost of a cleaner Charter. Recommended for the Board Charter (Category C below), not this instrument.

**B6 — No statute-of-limitations or staleness defence exists for removal/discipline grounds.** **Recorded, not amended this round.** A general limitation period cuts both ways — it could also shield a serious safeguarding matter that only comes to light years later, which this Charter should never do. This is better addressed case-by-case through the Disciplinary & Ethics Committee's own procedural fairness obligations (Article 23A's "reasonable opportunity to respond") than a blanket Charter-level time bar, and belongs in a future Statute governing that Committee's procedure rather than the Charter's own text.

**B7 — Article 80D's exclusion of the Shari'ah Advisory Council's Chairperson from a three-quarters override vote is ambiguous as to numerator versus denominator.** **Recorded, not amended this round** — genuinely a minor drafting-clarity point once Article 40's new co-opted-Governor rule (A10's fix) establishes the general pattern for how such exclusions are computed; the same interpretive principle now applies here by analogy without needing a third, near-duplicate clause.

**B8 — Article 78's Religious Affairs Coordination Council has no fallback if the Mudeer and Ra'ees simply decline to jointly convene a required meeting.** **Recorded, not amended this round.** Article 79's dispute-resolution route (referral to the Founder & CEO) already provides a real escalation path once a disagreement becomes apparent; a bare failure to convene is a discipline/performance matter for the office holders concerned, not a gap in the Council's own constitutional design.

**B9 — Article 43's "unanimous written consent" mechanism has no fallback if a single Governor is genuinely unreachable, not opposed.** **Recorded, not amended this round.** This is by design, not oversight: Article 21 already exists for genuinely time-critical unilateral action, and Article 149 for Board-quorum emergencies — Article 43 is better understood as a tool for swift *consensus* business, where requiring true unanimity is the point, not a flaw.

### Category C — Optional (belongs in the future Constitution, a Statute, a Regulation, or a Policy — not this Charter)

Consistent with the directive's own instruction not to overload the Governance Charter, and with Article 166's own stated principle that this Charter states constitutional principle while subordinate instruments state procedure, the following matters raised by this review's "think independently" checklist are deliberately **not** added to the Charter text:

- **Board meeting procedure at operational detail** (agenda templates, minute-taking standards, virtual-meeting protocol) — belongs in the Board Standing Orders / Board Charter (already identified as a genuine gap, Priority P1, in the companion `docs/shrs-institutional-documentation-framework.md`).
- **Conflict-of-interest register operational mechanics** (the form, the review cadence beyond "annual," how a declared interest is actually logged day to day) — belongs in the Conflict of Interest Policy (GV-05), not the Charter, which already correctly states the *principle* (Article 132) and *names* the register (Article 173).
- **Delegation thresholds in actual currency amounts** — belongs in the Delegation of Authority Framework (GV-04); the Charter correctly states only the *principle* that a threshold must exist and be periodically reviewed (Article 17).
- **Emergency operational detail** (evacuation routes, assembly points, specific incident-response playbooks) — already correctly lives in the Emergency Response Plan (SW-09), not the Charter, which correctly states only the constitutional *power* to act (Articles 21, 149).
- **Whistleblowing reporting-channel mechanics** (how a report is actually submitted, tracked, and acknowledged) — belongs in a Whistleblowing Policy (GV-06, already identified as missing in the companion documentation framework); the Charter correctly states only the *right* and its protection (Article 133).
- **A statute-of-limitations regime for discipline** (per B6 above) — belongs in a Statute governing the Disciplinary & Ethics Committee's own procedure, where case-specific nuance can actually be handled, not a blanket Charter-level rule.
- **The exact form of words for the constitutional affirmation** (Article 170) and **the Institution's crest/motto/seal specifics** (Schedule H) — the Charter already correctly defers these to Statute, and this review found no reason to change that judgment.

## STAGE TWO — INDEPENDENTLY IDENTIFIED, BEYOND THE ADVERSARIAL AGENT'S OWN LIST

Two further gaps were found by directly applying the directive's own missing-topics checklist to the full text, independent of the adversarial agent's 25 findings:

**Finding 26 (folded into B4 above) — "Repeal."** The directive's checklist explicitly names "repeal" as a topic to check. This Charter has no repeal mechanism for itself in the ordinary sense (a governing instrument does not typically provide for its own wholesale repeal without something to replace it) — but Article 178's future-Constitution provision is functionally the Charter's own repeal-by-supersession pathway, and it lacked a stated procedure, which is now fixed (A/B4 above).

**Finding 27 — Confirmed sound, no fix needed.** The interpretive hierarchy in Article 9B (Qur'an and Sunnah above the Charter, Nigerian law as a hard practical floor beneath both) was checked specifically for the scenario the directive's checklist implies — a genuine conflict between the two — and found already, deliberately, and correctly resolved: Article 9B requires independent Nigerian legal advice before acting and forbids an interpretation exposing the Institution to unlawful conduct under Nigerian law, while leaving the doctrinal hierarchy itself undisturbed. This is a sophisticated, working piece of drafting, not a gap, and this review's independent re-examination of it found nothing to change.

## STAGE THREE — SELF-CRITIQUE

Applying the directive's own standard — "assume another constitutional lawyer is trying to defeat your drafting in court" — three honest limits of this review, stated rather than left implicit:

1. **This review is not exhaustive against every possible bad-faith reading.** 178 Articles create a very large number of possible cross-reading combinations; this review traced the highest-stakes chains explicitly named in the directive (Founder incapacity scenarios, entrenchment workarounds, safeguarding quorum, emergency-power boundaries) and the ones the independent adversarial pass surfaced, but a sufficiently determined bad-faith actor with unlimited time might still find a reading this review did not anticipate. No governance instrument of this complexity can be certified as combinatorially complete by a single review pass, and this document does not claim that.
2. **Every fix in Category A is a targeted patch, not a re-architecture.** This was a deliberate choice, consistent with the directive's own Stage Four instruction not to rewrite everything — but it means each fix closes the specific gap found without a guarantee that the same underlying pattern (a power granted without a stated boundary; a threshold computed against an ambiguous base; a chain of dependent Articles) doesn't recur somewhere this review didn't specifically test.
3. **The Category B items left unfixed are a judgment call, not a certainty.** B5 and B6 in particular were left unamended on the reasoning that a general-purpose safeguard could create its own new problem (an attrition bootstrap-override that's itself exploitable; a limitations period that shields real misconduct) — but a differently-constituted review panel could reasonably reach the opposite conclusion on either. Both are recorded, not dismissed, precisely so the Board can revisit that judgment.

## STAGE FOUR — WHAT WAS AMENDED

Ten Category A fixes and four Category B fixes were applied directly to `docs/shrs-constitution-2026-draft.md`: Articles 14, 21, 23A, 26, 39, 40, 43, 49A, 49B, 134, 141A (new), 146A, 178, and 27C. Nothing else in the Charter's text was touched — every Article this review examined and found sound (the six-tier committee architecture in Chapter XI in full, the interpretive hierarchy in Article 9B, the tie-breaking rule in Article 40, the anti-self-dealing remuneration rule in Article 138A, the Safeguarding Committee's override power in Article 121) is preserved exactly as it stood. The document's Article numbering is preserved throughout except for the addition of Article 141A, inserted between Articles 141 and 142 using the Charter's own established letter-suffix convention (the same technique already used for Articles 9A, 9B, 23A, 27A–27F, 39A, 46A, 49A–49D, 80A–80H, 138A, 146A, 146B, 178A) — chosen deliberately over renumbering, for the same reason Drafting Note 7 gave when this technique was first adopted: an addition that doesn't require renumbering the whole instrument doesn't need to.

## CONCLUSION

Every Category A finding raised by an independent adversarial review, verified personally against the Charter's actual text before being accepted, has been fixed. The Category B items either have been fixed as well or are recorded with the specific reasoning for leaving them to a future Statute or the Board's own further judgment. No Category C matter has been added to the Charter, consistent with the directive's own instruction not to overload it — each belongs, and is named as belonging, in a subordinate instrument this Institution's own documentation framework already tracks.

**The Governance Charter is constitutionally mature and ready to serve as the permanent governance instrument of Sultan Hanafi Royal Schools. All remaining institutional matters belong in the forthcoming Constitution and subordinate instruments.**

That conclusion is this drafter's own, reached by the process this document describes in full — not a certification by King's Counsel, Oxford, Cambridge, Saudi Ministry of Education, UNESCO, or any accreditation body, none of which have reviewed this instrument. Real legal, regulatory, and Shari'ah-scholarly review remains necessary before the Board relies on this Charter or adopts it — the same caveat every document in this governance library has carried from the first page onward, and no less true here for being repeated once more.
